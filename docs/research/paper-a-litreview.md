# Paper A Literature Survey: Measuring JWT Verification Configuration at Scale

Literature the measurement paper "Authorization Contracts in the Wild: Measuring JWT Verification Configuration at Scale" (ACM SACMAT 2027) can cite and position against. This survey answers the earlier reviewer complaint that the related-work section did not cross-reference existing literature. It covers six threads: empirical web-auth and SSO measurement, large-scale misconfiguration measurement methodology, Infrastructure-as-Code and Kubernetes security, formal and attack analysis of OAuth/OIDC/JWT, config-as-contract and policy extraction, and the agent/MCP authorization frontier.

**Scope and method.** Candidate works were gathered via web search and each one was verified against a primary record (ACM Digital Library DOI, IEEE Xplore, USENIX proceedings, or the arXiv abstract page) on 2026-10-06. Titles, author lists, and venues are taken from those records. Works that could not be verified were excluded. BibTeX keys referenced here are defined in `paper-a-refs-new.bib`.

**Peer-review status.** Every peer-reviewed venue is named explicitly (CCS, IEEE S&P, USENIX Security, IMC, EuroS&P, CSF, ICSE, ASE, TOSEM, SecDev, ACSAC, NSDI, TDSC, FMSE, AISec). Works marked "preprint" are arXiv technical reports that are not yet peer-reviewed and should be cited as such. Tooling entries (Checkov, KICS, Terrascan, Semgrep, OPA, Pact) are industrial artifacts cited for context, not research claims.

## 1. Grouped survey

### Thread 1. Empirical and in-the-wild measurement of web authentication, SSO, OAuth, and OIDC

**Wang, Chen & Wang. Signing Me onto Your Accounts through Facebook and Google** [`authzmeas2012wangsignmein`, IEEE S&P 2012, peer-reviewed]. A traffic-guided security study of commercially deployed SSO that found eight serious logic flaws across identity providers and relying parties, each allowing an attacker to sign in as the victim. It established that SSO deployment flaws, not protocol flaws alone, are the dominant real-world risk. It inspects a handful of high-profile sites rather than measuring prevalence across a population.

**Sun & Beznosov. The Devil is in the (Implementation) Details: An Empirical Analysis of OAuth SSO Systems** [`authzmeas2012sunoauth`, ACM CCS 2012, peer-reviewed]. Examines three major OAuth identity providers and 96 popular relying-party websites, uncovering vulnerabilities that stem from design decisions trading security for implementation simplicity. The paper is a founding reference for the claim that insecure OAuth behaviour is driven by implementation and configuration choices. The scope is 96 hand-selected sites, not a measured population with confidence intervals.

**Zhou & Evans. SSOScan: Automated Testing of Web Applications for Single Sign-On Vulnerabilities** [`authzmeas2014ssoscan`, USENIX Security 2014, peer-reviewed]. Builds an automatic checker for five Facebook SSO vulnerabilities and runs it over the top 20,000 sites, finding that over 20 percent of the 1,660 sites using Facebook SSO had at least one serious flaw. It is the closest methodological ancestor to our work: automated detection of a fixed catalog of weaknesses across a crawled population. It tests runtime login behaviour via a driver rather than deriving a declared contract from static configuration.

**Mainka, Mladenov, Schwenk & Wich. SoK: Single Sign-On Security, An Evaluation of OpenID Connect** [`authzmeas2017soksso`, IEEE EuroS&P 2017, peer-reviewed]. Systematizes known SSO attacks, adapts them to OpenID Connect, introduces Identity Provider Confusion and Malicious Endpoints attacks, and reports that 75 percent of officially referenced OIDC libraries were vulnerable to at least one single-phase attack. It is the canonical SoK for OIDC attack classes and a reference for which weaknesses matter in OIDC. It evaluates libraries and a service-as-a-service harness rather than measuring configuration prevalence in the field.

**Ghasemisharif, Ramesh, Checkoway, Kanich & Polakis. O Single Sign-Off, Where Art Thou?** [`authzmeas2018singlesignoff`, USENIX Security 2018, peer-reviewed]. An empirical analysis of SSO account hijacking and session management that measured SSO support across the Alexa top one million (6.30 percent) and evaluated attacks against 95 major services. It couples a population-scale crawl with an attack study, a structure close to ours. Its subject is session and sign-off behaviour, not JWT verification settings.

**Jannett, Mladenov, Mainka & Schwenk. DISTINCT: Identity Theft using In-Browser Communications in Dual-Window SSO** [`authzmeas2022distinct`, ACM CCS 2022, peer-reviewed]. Translates SSO flows into sequence diagrams, flags insecure in-browser communication channels, and finds that 56 percent of the Tranco top 1k support dual-window SSO, with identity theft or XSS in 31 percent of self-implemented sites. It is a recent, rigorous in-the-wild SSO measurement with a clear prevalence result. It studies browser message passing in the login flow, a different layer from mesh and proxy verification config.

**Westers, Wich, Jannett, Mladenov, Mainka & Mayer. SSO-Monitor: Fully-Automatic Large-Scale Landscape, Security, and Privacy Analyses of Single Sign-On in the Wild** [`authzmeas2023ssomonitor`, arXiv preprint 2023]. A fully automated pipeline that discovers SSO logins across the Tranco 10k using visual detection and reports CSRF and other weaknesses at scale. It is the strongest statement of the "fully automatic, continuously updated, in-the-wild SSO measurement" goal that our population methodology shares. Preprint; a later systematization appeared as a peer-reviewed SoK. It measures live login flows, not declared verification contracts.

### Thread 2. Large-scale security misconfiguration measurement methodology

These works set the template our paper follows: measure the prevalence of a concrete weakness across a crawled population and report it with uncertainty.

**Durumeric, Kasten, Bailey & Halderman. Analysis of the HTTPS Certificate Ecosystem** [`authzmeas2013httpsecosystem`, ACM IMC 2013, peer-reviewed]. Reports 110 internet-wide scans over 14 months, classifies more than 1,800 certificate-issuing entities, and catalogs frequent configuration problems in the certificate ecosystem. It is a model for turning repeated population scans into a structured prevalence picture of a security-critical configuration surface. Its subject is TLS certificates rather than application-layer token verification.

**Durumeric, Li, Kasten, Amann, Beverly, Bailey, Halderman et al. The Matter of Heartbleed** [`authzmeas2014heartbleed`, ACM IMC 2014, peer-reviewed]. Measures the vulnerable population, tracks patching over time, and quantifies the certificate-ecosystem impact of a single concrete vulnerability. It demonstrates the measure-then-track-remediation structure and careful population accounting that a prevalence study should imitate. The weakness studied is a library bug rather than a declared policy setting.

**Durumeric, Adrian, Mirian, Kasten, Bursztein, Lidzborski, Thomas, Eranti, Bailey & Halderman. Neither Snow Nor Rain Nor MITM: An Empirical Analysis of Email Delivery Security** [`authzmeas2015emaildelivery`, ACM IMC 2015, peer-reviewed]. The first global report on adoption of SMTP security extensions, finding that of over 700,000 SMTP servers only 35 percent configure encryption and 1.1 percent set a DMARC policy. It is a direct precedent for measuring adoption of a declared security policy (SPF, DKIM, DMARC) across a large server population. The declared policies are email-authentication records rather than JWT verification rules.

**Felt, Barnes, King, Palmer, Bentzel & Tabriz. Measuring HTTPS Adoption on the Web** [`authzmeas2017httpsadoption`, USENIX Security 2017, peer-reviewed]. Benchmarks HTTPS adoption from both the browser-telemetry and the server-support perspectives across top and long-tail sites. It is a reference for reporting adoption of a security configuration from more than one vantage point, which parallels our declared-versus-effective distinction. Its surface is transport security, not authorization.

**Weichselbaum, Spagnuolo, Lekies & Janc. CSP Is Dead, Long Live CSP!** [`authzmeas2016csp`, ACM CCS 2016, peer-reviewed]. Analyzes real-world Content Security Policy deployments and finds that 94.68 percent of script-limiting policies are ineffective and 99.34 percent of CSP-using hosts gain no XSS benefit. It is a strong example of measuring how a declared security configuration fails in practice at population scale, then proposing a concrete fix. The declared object is a browser header, and the analysis is of a single header rather than a multi-field contract.

**Chen, Jiang, Duan, Wan, Chen, Paxson & Yang. We Still Don't Have Secure Cross-Domain Requests: An Empirical Study of CORS** [`authzmeas2018cors`, USENIX Security 2018, peer-reviewed]. An empirical study of CORS deployment showing that an inexpressive policy and subtle interactions with other mechanisms produce widespread misconfiguration. It is a close methodological peer: measuring how a per-service access-control policy is misconfigured across the web. The policy is cross-origin request permission, not token verification.

**Continella, Polino, Pogliani & Zanero. There's a Hole in that Bucket! A Large-scale Analysis of Misconfigured S3 Buckets** [`authzmeas2018s3buckets`, ACSAC 2018, peer-reviewed]. Audits 240,461 Amazon S3 buckets for access-control misconfigurations without exposing sensitive data. It shows how to measure cloud access-control misconfiguration prevalence responsibly and at scale. The configuration surface is object-storage ACLs rather than JWT verification in a service mesh.

### Thread 3. Infrastructure-as-Code, Kubernetes, and service-mesh security

**Rahman, Parnin & Williams. The Seven Sins: Security Smells in Infrastructure as Code Scripts** [`authzmeas2019sevensins`, ICSE 2019, peer-reviewed]. Derives seven security smells from 1,726 IaC scripts, builds the SLIC static analyzer, and finds 21,201 smell occurrences across 15,232 scripts from 293 repositories. It is the founding prevalence study of security weaknesses in declarative infrastructure code and the clearest precedent for mining a weakness catalog from a repository corpus. Its smells are generic IaC hygiene issues (hard-coded secrets, weak crypto), not authorization-contract fields.

**Rahman, Rahman, Parnin & Williams. Security Smells in Ansible and Chef Scripts: A Replication Study** [`authzmeas2021iacreplication`, ACM TOSEM 2021, peer-reviewed]. Replicates the smell methodology on 50,323 Ansible and Chef scripts from 813 repositories, adds two new smells, and reports 46,600 occurrences. It shows how a prevalence methodology is validated by replication across technologies and larger corpora, which supports our own recall and precision validation design. Same IaC-hygiene focus, not JWT or mesh policy.

**Saavedra & Ferreira. GLITCH: Automated Polyglot Security Smell Detection in Infrastructure as Code** [`authzmeas2022glitch`, ASE 2022, peer-reviewed]. Transforms Puppet, Ansible, and Chef scripts into a shared intermediate representation so one detector set applies across technologies, evaluated on 196,755 scripts with higher precision and recall than prior tools. Its intermediate-representation approach is a direct analogue to our normalization of heterogeneous mesh and proxy configuration into a single contract model. It targets generic smells rather than a typed authorization contract.

**Shamim, Bhuiyan & Rahman. XI Commandments of Kubernetes Security: A Systematization of Knowledge** [`authzmeas2020k8scommandments`, IEEE SecDev 2020, peer-reviewed]. Synthesizes eleven Kubernetes security practices from 104 internet artifacts, including RBAC least privilege and pod and network security policies. It is the orienting SoK for Kubernetes security practice and motivates why mesh and cluster configuration is a worthwhile measurement surface. It is a practice catalog from grey literature, not an in-the-wild measurement.

**Tooling context.** Config scanners are the practitioner-facing counterpart to this research. Checkov [`authzmeas2024checkov`], KICS [`authzmeas2024kics`], Terrascan [`authzmeas2024terrascan`], and Semgrep [`authzmeas2024semgrep`] apply rule sets to IaC and manifests, and Open Policy Agent [`authzmeas2024opa`] provides policy-as-code enforcement. These tools check rules against individual files. None derives a per-service JWT authorization contract or separates a declared contract from its effective enforcement, which is the gap our tool fills.

### Thread 4. Formal and attack analysis of OAuth, OIDC, SAML, and JWT

These works ground which weaknesses matter: they prove, by formal analysis or concrete attack, that specific issuer, audience, algorithm, and endpoint behaviours are security-relevant.

**Armando, Carbone, Compagna, Cuellar & Tobarra. Formal Analysis of SAML 2.0 Web Browser Single Sign-On: Breaking the SAML-based Single Sign-on for Google Apps** [`authzmeas2008samlsso`, ACM FMSE 2008, peer-reviewed]. A model-checking analysis that found an authentication flaw in the SAML SSO profile as deployed by Google Apps. It is an early demonstration that a deployment-specific binding mistake, not a protocol-level flaw, breaks SSO. It analyzes SAML rather than OAuth or JWT.

**Bansal, Bhargavan, Delignat-Lavaud & Maffeis. Discovering Concrete Attacks on Website Authorization by Formal Analysis** [`authzmeas2012websanalysis`, IEEE CSF 2012; extended in Journal of Computer Security 2014, peer-reviewed]. Models OAuth 2.0 configurations in the applied pi-calculus with the WebSpi library and ProVerif, accounting for CSRF and open redirectors, and uses the model to find many previously unknown vulnerabilities in real sites. It links formal authorization properties to concrete, configuration-dependent attacks. The analysis is of protocol flows, not a measured deployment population.

**Fett, Kuesters & Schmitz. A Comprehensive Formal Security Analysis of OAuth 2.0** [`authzmeas2016oauthformal`, ACM CCS 2016, peer-reviewed]. Builds an expressive web model, finds four attacks that break OAuth and also affect OpenID Connect, and proves authorization, authentication, and session-integrity properties for a fixed version. It is the authoritative formal statement of what correct OAuth must guarantee, including audience and redirect handling. It defines the properties a correct contract should encode but does not measure contracts in the field.

**Fett, Kuesters & Schmitz. The Web SSO Standard OpenID Connect: In-Depth Formal Security Analysis and Security Guidelines** [`authzmeas2017oidcformal`, IEEE CSF 2017, peer-reviewed]. Formalizes authentication, authorization, and session-integrity for OpenID Connect and proves they hold in the model, yielding concrete security guidelines. It is the companion authority for OIDC and a source for which issuer and token-binding requirements are load-bearing. Again formal and prescriptive, not a measurement.

**JWT weaknesses, practitioner record.** The concrete JWT weaknesses our paper counts (the `alg=none` acceptance and HMAC-versus-RSA key confusion) were first widely documented by McLean [`authzmeas2015jwtlibs`] and are catalogued by the OWASP and PortSwigger JWT references [`authzmeas2024portswiggerjwt`]. These are industrial writeups rather than peer-reviewed research, so our paper cites them for the weakness definitions and relies on RFC 8725 (JWT Best Current Practices) [`rfc8725`] for the normative requirement each weakness violates.

### Thread 5. Config-as-contract, declarative-policy extraction, and contract testing

**Fogel, Fung, Pedrosa, Walraed-Sullivan, Govindan, Mahajan & Millstein. A General Approach to Network Configuration Analysis (Batfish)** [`authzmeas2015batfish`, USENIX NSDI 2015, peer-reviewed]. Parses heterogeneous device configuration files into a vendor-independent model, then checks reachability and policy properties against that model. It is the closest conceptual precedent for our work: derive a behavioural or policy model from static deployment configuration and reason about it, rather than observing runtime traffic. It targets network forwarding and routing, not application authorization, and verifies rather than measuring prevalence across a population.

**Xu & Stoller. Mining Attribute-Based Access Control Policies** [`authzmeas2015abacmining`, IEEE TDSC 2015, peer-reviewed]. Infers a compact ABAC policy from existing access-control data and attribute information, partially automating migration to attribute-based access control. It is the reference for extracting a declarative authorization policy from lower-level artifacts, which parallels deriving a contract from configuration. It mines from access logs and ACLs rather than from mesh and proxy config, and does not measure deployed populations.

**Contract testing, tooling.** Consumer-driven contract testing, represented by Pact [`authzmeas2024pact`], derives and verifies interface contracts between services. It is the closest named analogue to "config as contract" in practice. Pact contracts are request and response shapes agreed between a consumer and provider, not authorization properties recovered from deployment configuration, and the approach is test-driven rather than a population measurement.

### Thread 6. Agent and MCP authorization, the forward frontier

Three anchor works show the same measure-and-analyze methodology moving to agent infrastructure, where our declared-contract framing applies next.

**Greshake, Abdelnabi, Mishra, Endres, Holz & Fritz. Not What You've Signed Up For: Compromising Real-World LLM-Integrated Applications with Indirect Prompt Injection** [`authzmeas2023promptinjection`, ACM AISec 2023, peer-reviewed]. The seminal indirect-prompt-injection paper, showing that adversaries can plant instructions in data a model later retrieves and compromise real applications. It is the threat anchor that makes token-scoped, verifiable authorization necessary for agents. It is an attack demonstration, not a measurement.

**Zhou, Zhang, Zhang, Zhang, Zhang & Yang. A First Measurement Study on Authentication Security in Real-World Remote MCP Servers** [`authzmeas2026mcpauthmeas`, arXiv preprint 2026]. Identifies 7,973 live remote MCP servers, finds 40.55 percent expose tools without authentication, and reports that every one of 119 testable OAuth-enabled servers has at least one flaw, with dynamic client registration flaws affecting 96.6 percent. It is the direct extension of population-scale authentication measurement into the agent ecosystem. Preprint.

**Padilla. Exposed by Design: A Dynamic Security Assessment of Internet-Facing MCP Servers at Scale** [`authzmeas2026mcpexposed`, arXiv preprint 2026]. Discovers more than 21,000 MCP server instances, confirms 640 and dynamically audits 414, finding that 91.8 percent lack OAuth authentication and many expose shell execution without access control. It shows the measure-at-scale template applied to agent infrastructure with a dynamic testing harness. Preprint.

## 2. How Paper A positions against each thread and the gap it fills

**The gap.** No prior in-the-wild study measures JWT verification configuration across a population. Paper A derives, from public deployment configuration (Istio RequestAuthentication and AuthorizationPolicy, Envoy jwt_authn, OIDC), each service's declared authorization contract, which is its trusted issuers, bound audiences, allowed algorithms, required claims, and JWKS behaviour, and measures how prevalent concrete RFC-cited weaknesses are across 2,718 repositories and 530 JWT-validating configurations, with Wilson confidence intervals and a validation methodology (independent-parse recall oracle, negative-control precision, held-out over-attribution, and a human gold-standard sample). The contribution is population-scale measurement of the declared authorization contract recovered from mesh and proxy configuration, with an explicit separation of the declared contract from its effective enforcement.

**Against Thread 1 (web-auth and SSO measurement).** This thread measures live login and session behaviour by driving flows (SSOScan, O Single Sign-Off, DISTINCT, SSO-Monitor) or inspects a small set of sites by hand (Wang et al., Sun and Beznosov). Paper A keeps the population-scale ambition and the fixed weakness catalog but changes the object and the vantage point: it reads the declared verification contract out of static mesh and proxy configuration instead of exercising a runtime flow, which lets it cover services that no crawler can log into and separate what a service declares from what it enforces.

**Against Threads 2 and 3 (misconfiguration measurement and IaC security).** These threads provide the methodological template, which is measuring the prevalence of a concrete weakness across a crawled population and reporting it carefully (HTTPS, CSP, CORS, S3, email), and the repository-mining precedent for config weaknesses (Seven Sins, GLITCH, the Ansible and Chef replication). Paper A adopts that template, including confidence intervals and a replication-style validation of recall and precision, and applies it to a surface none of them touches, which is the JWT authorization contract. Where IaC smell detectors check generic rules against single files, Paper A normalizes heterogeneous config into one typed contract per service and reasons over it, closer in spirit to Batfish than to a linter.

**Against Threads 4, 5, and 6 (formal analysis, config-as-contract, agents).** The formal and attack literature (Fett et al., Bansal et al., Armando et al., Mainka et al.) establishes which issuer, audience, algorithm, and endpoint behaviours are security-relevant, so Paper A does not re-argue that the weaknesses matter; it cites these works for the properties and then measures how often real deployments violate them. Batfish and ABAC policy mining show that deriving a policy model from configuration or data is an accepted method, but neither recovers a JWT authorization contract or measures it across a population. The agent and MCP measurement work shows the same methodology reaching the next frontier, where Paper A's declared-versus-effective contract framing is the natural lens to carry forward.
